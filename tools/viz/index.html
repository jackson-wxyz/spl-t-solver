SPL-T Clockwork

How a computer-found game of SPL-T outlasts every score on record: four columns of point blocks kept in a halving chain, a beam search that steers the opening by how long a simple policy would survive, and from move 700 a second search that asks only one thing of a position: how many levels deep its four columns are.

The clockwork: four pipes, one value halving down each

Every column pair is a pipe holding a stack of up to eight 2×2 clusters. A cluster is born at the top worth the current move number and is halved each time the cluster beneath it explodes and it falls one slot. Dark cells are high values; each pipe reads as a staircase that drifts down and to the right. Hover for the exact chain at any move.

The search's safety margin

The evaluation that made the opening possible is blunt: from every candidate position, play the hand-tuned heuristic greedily and count how many moves it survives. The chart shows that count along the final game. For the first 700 moves the search kept picking positions where that margin stayed above 200. At move 700 a different search takes over (see "The pipe keeper" below) and the margin drops to between 20 and 120 for the rest of the game. Those are positions the greedy heuristic cannot play: every pipe is full, and there is no slack for a policy that does not know the schedule.

A full board is a healthy board

The first intuition, rewarding free space, was backwards. From move 700 on, 108 to 120 of the 128 cells are point blocks and only three or four blocks can legally be split, yet the game has over three thousand moves left. Free cells are the raw material being turned into the next cluster; everything else is the clockwork running.

How often the search overruled the heuristic

Each child of a beam node is one of the heuristic's four best-rated moves. In Josh Holder's computed games of 2022 the heuristic's first choice was played 98% of the time; in the first 700 moves of this game it was played 57% of the time, and in the opening less than half. From move 700 the search does not consult the heuristic at all. Its move is still the heuristic's first choice 57% of the time and one of its top four 97% of the time: with three legal moves on average there is not much room to disagree, and the disagreements are where the game is decided.

Many runs: the same recipe, wildly different games

The search is deterministic, so a seed only adds tiny noise to the scores, yet the same setting lands anywhere from about 1,050 to 1,900. Each run is a chaotic path through the opening; the width of the beam and the rollout cap shift the whole distribution, and every penalty added on top of the plain rollout length made it worse.

The ratchet and the local search: three pipes and a store

Before the keeper, the long games came from a second search layered on top of the rollout beam. None of its moves are in the best game any more, but it is how the structure of a long game was found. The ratchet takes the best game so far, cuts it some number of moves before the end, runs a fresh beam from there with a new seed, and keeps the result only if it is at least as long. Cuts less than about 100 moves from the end never change the length (the last 70 moves of a game are already fixed by how much splitting capacity is on the board); cuts 120 to 400 back produce the gains. Keeping a pool of the eight longest games and varying each attempt's settings took a 2,064 game to 2,670, and then stalled.

The local search got past that. Its rollouts do not ask the heuristic what to do: they replay the move the best game itself made at the same point, whenever the same block is still there. A position on the game is then worth exactly what the game went on to score, and a position that deviates is judged by whether it can get back onto the game's schedule and what that does to the ending. Each step kicks the game (plays a different move somewhere in its last 450 moves, which usually costs dozens of moves), then searches for a repair. A step takes about a second, and now and then the repaired game is longer. It passed 3,000 within an hour, partly by discovering that two neighbouring pipes can be made to explode on the same move, which refills them with one wide block worth seven splits instead of six. It stalled at 3,012 until one detail changed: when a rollout has to leave the game's schedule it now picks a block the game is not about to split, in practice one in the fourth pipe, so the three working pipes keep their rhythm. That added 91 moves in eleven minutes, and five more hours added 37. These games run three pipes at full depth and use the fourth as a store: spare splits go into clusters in the wrong place there, which come back hundreds of moves later. The store is what carries them past the point where three pipes stop keeping up, and it is why they end near 3,100.

The pipe keeper: all four pipes, and no rollouts

The best game comes from a much simpler search. It is a beam over every legal move that never plays a game out. It scores a position by counting, for each pipe, how many separate explosions its stack of clusters will produce: two clusters that will reach the bottom together count once, and a pipe that is waiting for its next cluster gets one for it in advance. That count is the pipe's depth, eight at most. The beam keeps the positions with the greatest total depth, refuses clusters that are not on top of a stack, and among positions of equal depth prefers those in which two neighbouring pipes will explode on the same move.

A board with more depth than the move number needs gets more splits back than it can spend, and a full board has nowhere to keep them. The rollout searches let one pipe absorb the surplus and lost that pipe. The keeper burns the surplus instead: it turns a refill block into a cluster of four 2×1 blocks, which takes three splits where the ordinary route takes seven and throws the other four away. The best game does that about 150 times after move 700 and loses 632 splits to it. In exchange, every pipe is eight clusters deep from about move 2,510 to the end, and the depth is there when the move number catches up with it.

Two runs of eight seconds each produced the game: one from move 700 of the earlier best game (3,588 splits), and one from move 2,600 of that result with the bonus for shared explosions switched on. Where a keeper game ends follows from what it has built. An eight-deep pipe explodes about every n/265 moves at move n and each explosion returns three splits, or seven for two pipes that explode together, so a structure keeps up until n/265 equals what it returns per period. The games last up to about 200 moves beyond that point on the few spare splits a full board holds.

Two things it does not do. Nine runs in ten from an early move die within 150 moves, because the position they take over still holds the old game's store and the keeper will not touch it. And nothing tried so far improves a finished keeper game: from move 3,300 on, every setting ends on exactly 3,898, and the local search of the previous section finds no repair for a game whose cycle has no slack.

The beam's family tree

A beam keeps the best thirty (here) or hundred positions at every move and drops the rest. Watching which lineages survive shows the search for what it is: long stretches where every kept position descends from one ancestor a few dozen moves back, punctuated by moments where two or three lineages compete before one wins. The top chart counts how many distinct ancestors, thirty moves back, the thirty kept positions still have; the lower one draws the tree itself for a window you can slide.

How long could a game be?

Two facts set a ceiling. Splitting capacity is conserved: an exploding 2×2 cluster refills a 2×2 block worth exactly three splits, so the four pipes together must deliver an explosion about every three moves, or each pipe every twelve. And a cluster is two rows tall, so no block can have more than seven clusters beneath it, which caps it at seven halvings. A fresh cluster worth n reaches the bottom worth about n/128, so the interval between a deep pipe's explosions grows in proportion to the move number. The chart simulates one pipe and shows where it can no longer keep up.

An eight-deep pipe falls behind around move 3,200 with plain refills and around 4,000 with perfect ones, and the slack left on the board stretches that by roughly ten percent. So no game can pass about 4,700 splits, and a perfect four-pipe clockwork tops out near 3,800. Josh Holder's own estimates were 3,700 in theory and about 1,800 in practice. My guess for the real maximum used to be around 3,000, because an eight-deep pipe leaves no room for timing errors, every halving rounds down, and the forced alternation of split directions keeps fighting the staggering of the four pipes. That guess described the rollout searches, not the game. Two neighbouring pipes that explode on the same move refill as one 4×2 block worth seven splits, not two blocks worth six, so with both pairs of pipes doing that each pipe can afford fourteen moves between explosions, which holds until about move 3,700. The best game is that clockwork: two pairs, half a period apart, ending 188 moves past that point. All four pipes in unison would return fifteen, but their one 8×2 block can only be turned into clusters with 3, 7, 11 or 15 splits, and the interval is whatever the cluster values make it, so that structure cannot run by itself.
